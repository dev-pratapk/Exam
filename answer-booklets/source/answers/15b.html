Statement

Let (B, +, ·, ′, 0, 1) be a Boolean algebra. Then for all a, b ∈ B,

(i)  (a + b)′ = a′ · b′,      (ii)  (a · b)′ = a′ + b′.

In words: the complement of a sum is the product of the complements, and the complement of a product is the sum of the complements.

Axioms and results used

  • Commutative laws: a + b = b + a, a · b = b · a.
  • Distributive laws: a + (b · c) = (a + b) · (a + c), a · (b + c) = (a · b) + (a · c).
  • Complement laws: a + a′ = 1, a · a′ = 0.
  • Identity and domination laws: a + 0 = a, a · 1 = a, a + 1 = 1, a · 0 = 0.

Lemma: the complement is unique

Suppose an element x has two complements c and d, that is x + c = 1, x · c = 0 and x + d = 1, x · d = 0. Then

c = c · 1 = c · (x + d) = (c · x) + (c · d) = 0 + (c · d) = c · d,

so c ≤ d (since c · d = c). Interchanging c and d gives d ≤ c. Hence c = d, so the complement of any element is unique.

Proof of (i)

Put c = a′ · b′. It is enough to show that c is a complement of a + b; by the lemma there is then only one complement, so (a + b)′ = c.

Step 1 (the sum is 1). Using the distributive law x + y·z = (x + y)·(x + z),

(a + b) + c = (a + b) + a′b′ = (a + b + a′)(a + b + b′) = (1 + b)(a + 1) = 1 · 1 = 1.

Step 2 (the product is 0).

(a + b) · c = (a + b) · a′b′ = a·a′·b′ + b·a′·b′ = (a·a′)b′ + (b·b′)a′ = 0·b′ + 0·a′ = 0.

Thus (a + b) + c = 1 and (a + b) · c = 0, so c = a′b′ is the complement of a + b. By uniqueness,

(a + b)′ = a′ · b′.

Proof of (ii)

A Boolean algebra is self-dual: the dual of any true statement, obtained by interchanging + with · and 0 with 1 (leaving ′, a, b unchanged), is also true, because every axiom has its dual among the axioms. Applying duality to law (i) gives immediately

(a · b)′ = a′ + b′.

Direct check with d = a′ + b′: on one hand (a·b) + d = ab + a′ + b′ = (a + a′)(b + a′) + b′ = 1·(b + a′) + b′ = a′ + (b + b′) = a′ + 1 = 1; on the other hand (a·b)·d = ab(a′ + b′) = aa′b + abb′ = 0 + 0 = 0. Hence d is the complement of a·b.

Hence in every Boolean algebra (a + b)′ = a′·b′ and (a·b)′ = a′ + b′. These are De Morgan's laws. ∎

Given

D30 = {1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30} with x ≤ y ⇔ x divides y (written x | y). This is the divisibility poset of the divisors of 30; in it a ∧ b = gcd(a, b) and a ∨ b = lcm(a, b).

Definitions used

  • Lower bound of a and b: an element l with l ≤ a and l ≤ b, i.e. l | a and l | b.
  • GLB (greatest lower bound): the lower bound that is divisible by every other lower bound.
  • Upper bound of a and b: an element u with a ≤ u and b ≤ u, i.e. a | u and b | u.
  • LUB (least upper bound): the upper bound that divides every other upper bound.

(1) All lower bounds of 10 and 15

Divisors of 10 in D30: 1, 2, 5, 10. Divisors of 15: 1, 3, 5, 15. The common divisors, which divide both 10 and 15, are 1 and 5.

Lower bounds of 10 and 15 = {1, 5}.

(2) GLB of 10 and 15

Of the lower bounds 1 and 5, we have 1 | 5, so 5 is the greatest (it is divisible by 1).

GLB(10, 15) = 5 = gcd(10, 15).

(3) All upper bounds of 10 and 15

Multiples of 10 in D30: 10, 30. Multiples of 15: 15, 30. The common multiples are 30 only.

Upper bounds of 10 and 15 = {30}.

(4) LUB of 10 and 15

LUB(10, 15) = 30 = lcm(10, 15).

(5) Hasse diagram of D30

Since 30 is a product of three distinct primes, D30 is isomorphic to the Boolean lattice B3 of subsets of a 3-element set, the correspondence being x ↔ the set of prime divisors of x:

1 ↔ ∅, 2 ↔ {2}, 3 ↔ {3}, 5 ↔ {5}, 6 ↔ {2,3}, 10 ↔ {2,5}, 15 ↔ {3,5}, 30 ↔ {2,3,5}.

Hence its Hasse diagram is a cube: 1 at the bottom, the atoms 2, 3, 5 on the first level, the elements 6, 10, 15 on the second level, and 30 at the top.

Lower bounds = {1, 5}; GLB = 5. Upper bounds = {30}; LUB = 30. The Hasse diagram of D30 is a cube. ∎
