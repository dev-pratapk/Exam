Equivalence of p → q and ¬p ∨ q

The conditional p → q is false only in the case p true, q false. Evaluating both propositions in all four cases:

pqp → q¬p¬p ∨ q
TTTFT
TFFFF
FTTTT
FFTTT

The columns for p → q and ¬p ∨ q agree in every row.

Hence p → q ⇔ ¬p ∨ q; the two propositions are logically equivalent. ∎

Symbolizing "All men are mortal"

Let the universe of discourse be the set of all human beings, and define the predicates

  • M(x): x is a man,
  • D(x): x is mortal.

"All men are mortal" means: for every x, if x is a man then x is mortal. Hence the statement is symbolized as

∀x (M(x) → D(x)).
The symbolization is ∀x (M(x) → D(x)); if the universe is taken as the set of men only, it reduces to ∀x D(x). ∎

Pigeonhole Principle

Statement. If n pigeons are placed in m pigeonholes and n > m, then at least one pigeonhole contains more than one pigeon (at least two pigeons).

Generalized form. If n objects are distributed among m boxes, then at least one box contains at least ⌈n/m⌉ objects, where ⌈x⌉ denotes the least integer ≥ x.

Thus placing n > m objects into m boxes forces some box to contain at least ⌈n/m⌉ ≥ 2 objects. ∎

Number of bit strings of length 9

Method (product rule). A bit string of length 9 has 9 positions. Each position can be filled independently in 2 ways, either by 0 or by 1.

Number of strings = 2 × 2 × … (9 times) = 29 = 512.
There are 29 = 512 different bit strings of length 9. ∎

Simple graph with degree sequence {3, 3, 3, 3, 2}

Sum of degrees = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 14, which is even, so the handshaking lemma raises no objection. Apply the Havel–Hakimi algorithm to the sequence (3, 3, 3, 3, 2):

  1. Remove 3 and subtract 1 from the next three entries: (2, 2, 2, 2).
  2. Remove 2 and subtract 1 from the next two entries: (1, 1, 2), i.e. (2, 1, 1).
  3. Remove 2 and subtract 1 from the next two entries: (0, 0), all zeros.

All zeros are reached, so the sequence is graphical. An explicit graph has edges

v1v2, v1v3, v1v4, v2v3, v2v4, v3v5, v4v5.
Yes. A simple graph with degree sequence {3, 3, 3, 3, 2} exists; it has 7 edges and 5 vertices.

Uniqueness of the identity element of a group

Given: a group ⟨G, *⟩. To prove: G has exactly one identity element.

Suppose e and e′ are both identity elements of G. Since e′ is an identity, acting on the left element e gives

e * e′ = e.

Since e is an identity, acting on the right element e′ gives

e * e′ = e′.

The left sides are equal, so e = e′.

Hence the identity element of a group is unique. ∎

Field

Definition. A field is a non-empty set F with two binary operations + (addition) and * (multiplication) such that:

  1. (F, +) is an abelian group with identity 0 (additive identity) and additive inverse −a for each a;
  2. (F − {0}, *) is an abelian group with identity 1 (≠ 0) and multiplicative inverse a−1 for each a ≠ 0;
  3. Multiplication distributes over addition: a * (b + c) = (a * b) + (a * c) for all a, b, c ∈ F.

Equivalently, a field is a commutative ring with unity in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. The sets ℝ, ℚ and ℂ under ordinary addition and multiplication are fields.

A field is a commutative division ring: both (F, +) and (F − {0}, *) are abelian groups and * distributes over +. ∎

De Morgan's laws in a Boolean algebra

Statement. In any Boolean algebra ⟨B, +, ·, ′, 0, 1⟩, for all a, b ∈ B the complements satisfy:

(a + b)′ = a′ · b′    and    (a · b)′ = a′ + b′.

Here + denotes join (OR), · denotes meet (AND), and ′ denotes complement (NOT).

De Morgan's laws: the complement of a sum is the product of the complements, and the complement of a product is the sum of the complements. ∎
