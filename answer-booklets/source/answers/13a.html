Given

Let G = (V, E) be a finite undirected graph (no loops). For each vertex v, let d(v) denote its degree, and let

V1 = { v ∈ V : d(v) is odd },    V2 = { v ∈ V : d(v) is even }.

To prove: the number of vertices in V1 (that is, the number of odd-degree vertices) is even.

Definitions and result used

  • Degree d(v): the number of edges incident with the vertex v.
  • Handshaking lemma: in any graph G = (V, E), the sum of the degrees of all the vertices equals twice the number of edges:
    Σv∈V d(v) = 2 |E|.
    Each edge has two ends, so it contributes 1 to the degree of each of its two end vertices; hence it is counted twice in the sum.

Proof

Let n1 = |V1| and n2 = |V2|. Every vertex has a degree that is either odd or even, so V1 and V2 are disjoint and V1 ∪ V2 = V.

  1. Split the degree sum. Since V1 and V2 partition V,
    Σv∈V d(v) = Σv∈V1 d(v) + Σv∈V2 d(v).
  2. Use the handshaking lemma. The left side equals 2|E|, which is an even integer:
    2 |E| = Σv∈V1 d(v) + Σv∈V2 d(v).
  3. The even-degree part is even. Each term d(v) in Σv∈V2 d(v) is even, and a sum of even integers is even. So Σv∈V2 d(v) is even.
  4. The odd-degree part is even. From step 2,
    Σv∈V1 d(v) = 2 |E| − Σv∈V2 d(v).
    This is the difference of two even integers, hence it is even.
  5. Count the odd terms. The sum Σv∈V1 d(v) has exactly n1 terms, and every term is odd. A sum of n1 odd integers is odd when n1 is odd and even when n1 is even; in other words, the parity of the sum equals the parity of n1.
  6. Conclude. By step 4 the sum is even, so by step 5 the number n1 of terms must be even.

Illustration. In a graph with degree sequence (3, 3, 2, 2, 2) the odd degrees are 3 and 3, so there are exactly two odd-degree vertices, an even number. A sequence such as (3, 3, 3) can never occur as the degree sequence of a graph, because it has three odd entries.

Hence the number of vertices of odd degree in any graph G is always even. ∎

Reasoning

An isomorphism f : V(G1) → V(G2) is a bijection that preserves adjacency and non-adjacency. Because degree is preserved, f must map the four degree-3 vertices of G1 bijectively onto the four degree-3 vertices of G2. Moreover, if two degree-3 vertices are joined by an edge in G1, their images must be joined by an edge in G2, and conversely. Therefore the subgraph induced by the degree-3 vertices of G1 must be isomorphic to the subgraph induced by the degree-3 vertices of G2.

In G1, the only edges with both ends of degree 3 are u2u6 and u4u8; these are two disjoint edges, so the induced subgraph is 2K2 (it has 2 edges). In G2, the edges with both ends of degree 3 are v1v2, v2v6, v6v5, v5v1, which form the 4-cycle v1–v2–v6–v5–v1 (it has 4 edges). The two induced subgraphs have different numbers of edges, hence are not isomorphic.

Equivalently, G2 contains a 4-cycle on its four degree-3 vertices while G1 contains no such cycle; an isomorphism would have to carry one to the other, which is impossible.

Hence G1 and G2 are not isomorphic, even though they have the same number of vertices, the same number of edges and the same degree sequence. ∎
