Introduction

In group communication a message is multicast to all members of a group. The ordering guarantees are: unordered, FIFO, causal and total order. Total order means that if two messages Mx and My are delivered to both processes pi and pj, they are delivered in the same order at both: every member sees the same sequence of messages. It is needed, for example, in replicated databases and replicated state machines, where all copies must apply updates in the same order.

1. Centralized algorithm

One process of the group acts as the coordinator (sequencer).

  1. When pi wants to multicast a message M to group G, it sends M to the coordinator.
  2. The coordinator receives messages one by one and sends each M to all members of G, in the order in which it received them.
  3. Each member delivers the messages in the order in which they arrive from the coordinator.

Complexity: for a group of n members, about n + 1 messages per multicast (one to the coordinator and n from it) and two message hops of delay.

Disadvantages: the coordinator is a single point of failure and a bottleneck; if it fails, a new coordinator must be elected.

2. Distributed (three-phase) algorithm

No coordinator is used. Each process pj keeps a counter tsnj (the next timestamp number) and two queues: temp_Q, holding messages with a timestamp and a flag deliverable / undeliverable, sorted by timestamp, and delivery_Q, from which messages are delivered. Messages with equal timestamps are ordered by the sender’s id.

Phase 1: the sender asks for a timestamp.

  1. pi sends the message M to all members of the group G.
  2. Each member pj that receives M does tsnj := tsnj + 1, assigns this value as the temporary timestamp of M, puts (M, tsnj, undeliverable) in temp_Q and sends REVISE_TS(M, tsnj) back to pi.

Phase 2: the sender decides the final timestamp.

  1. When pi has received REVISE_TS from every member, it computes the final timestamp sn = max of all the tsnj and sends FINAL_TS(M, sn) to all members.

Phase 3: delivery.

  1. A member that receives FINAL_TS(M, sn) finds M in temp_Q, sets its timestamp to sn, marks it deliverable and re-sorts temp_Q by timestamp.
  2. While the message at the head of temp_Q is deliverable, it is moved to delivery_Q and delivered. A deliverable message behind an undeliverable message at the head must wait.

Example: two concurrent multicasts

P1 multicasts A and P2 multicasts B at the same time to the group {P1, P2, P3}. All counters start at 0. The receive orders differ: P1 and P3 receive A before B, and P2 receives B before A.

Both final timestamps are 2, and the tie is broken by sender id: A (sender P1) comes before B (sender P2). At P2, temp_Q is first {B(1, undeliverable), A(2, undeliverable)}. When FINAL_TS(B) = 2 arrives, B becomes deliverable but the queue is re-sorted and A (2, from P1) is now at the head and still undeliverable, so B must wait. When FINAL_TS(A) = 2 arrives, A is delivered and then B. P1 and P3 also deliver A then B. All three deliver A, B in the same order.

Correctness

  • The final timestamp of a message is decided once, by its sender, and the same value is sent to all members, so every member sorts the messages in the same order.
  • A temporary timestamp is never larger than the final one. A message is delivered only when it is at the head of temp_Q and deliverable, so no message with an undecided final timestamp can end up before it.

Complexity: 3(n − 1) messages per multicast (n − 1 for M, n − 1 for REVISE_TS and n − 1 for FINAL_TS), and three message hops of delay.

Hence total order can be achieved by a centralized sequencer (about n + 1 messages, but a single point of failure) or by the distributed three-phase algorithm (3(n − 1) messages, no coordinator), and in both every member delivers the messages in the same order.
